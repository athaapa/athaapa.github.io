I've been working on [mohg](https://github.com/athaapa/mohg), a polyphonic synthesizer in Odin for macOS. I wanted to learn real-time audio and performance engineering under a hard deadline, and a synth seemed like a good way to do that while having something I could actually play. I'm using an Arturia MiniLab 3 for MIDI input and an M4 MacBook Air.

The goal was to render a 64-frame stereo buffer at 48 kHz. Those 64 frames are

$$
\frac{64}{48000} \approx 1.333\text{ ms}
$$

of audio, so that's my time budget for each render callback. I set October 24 as the end date so I'd have to pick a scope and finish.

My benchmark workload is 32 voices with 16 unison oscillators each, a nonlinear ladder filter on every voice, and 4x oversampling. When I first added oversampling, that workload took 1209.89 µs per block, or about 91% of the budget. After adding some heavier sound features and making three optimizations, the first 32-voice run in the final sweep took 670.01 µs per block (about 50%), with a p99 of 751.58 µs and no blocks over budget in 5000 measured blocks. Measured by where the mean block time crosses the budget, 4x capacity went from about 35 voices to about 59.

| Change | Mean before, µs/block | Mean after, µs/block | Effect |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cache per-note oscillator values | 1209.89 | 519.33 | 2.33x faster |
| Replace `tanh` with an approximation | 1127.36 | 639.95 | 1.76x faster |
| Only compute decimator outputs I keep | 723.34 | 668.48 | 7.6% less time |

I added features between these experiments, so each row compares two runs on the same version, and the rows don't multiply into one overall speedup.

### Capacity

My early benchmark didn't count overruns yet. It reported the mean and the share of the budget it used, so to compare against those runs I used the same measure on both sides: the voice count where the mean block time crosses 1333.33 µs. I interpolated linearly between the two measured voice counts on either side of the budget, using the sweep from my first oversampled version (before caching) for the old numbers.

| Oversampling | Before caching, voices | Final sweep, voices |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1x | ~119 | ~117 |
| 2x | ~67 | ~78 |
| 4x | ~35 | ~59 |

My notes from that time estimated 115-116, 64-65, and 34-35, which are close to the interpolated values. At 4x, capacity went up by about 24 voices. At 1x it barely moved. In between, I added saturation in every ladder stage, drift, a saturating mixer, and noise, so my reading is that the optimizations roughly paid for those features at 1x and bought extra headroom at 4x.

The final sweep also shows that 2x costs a lot less than twice 1x: 415.91 µs against 339.02 µs at 32 voices. The decimator still runs its full 257-tap filter on every output frame at 1x, and my guess is that this fixed cost is part of the reason, but I haven't measured it separately.

### Overruns below the budget

I also wanted the count from my original goal: the maximum number of voices with no overruns. I tested counts near each boundary and required three runs in a row with zero overruns. The highest counts that passed were 61 voices at 1x, 46 at 2x, and 24 at 4x.

Those counts are much lower than the mean crossings, and they don't line up with the load. At 4x, 48 voices passed its first run with no overruns, but 34 and 32 voices failed later and 24 was the count that passed. At 4x, 25 voices averaged 593.17 µs, under half the budget, and still had 25 blocks over it. In the right panel, overruns show up all over the range below the dotted lines, and only become nearly every block once the mean crosses the budget.

The same configuration also got slower over the sweep. The first 4x, 32-voice run averaged 670.01 µs. The same executable and settings averaged 743.29 µs later in the same sweep, with 34 overruns and a p99 of 1189.33 µs. The 4x, 48-voice run went from 1012.50 µs to 1107.90 µs. The refinement runs at 4x were the last ones in the sweep.

My two guesses were heat and the OS pausing the thread. The MacBook Air doesn't have a fan, so a long sweep could heat the chip until it lowers its clock speed, which would make every block a little slower. The benchmark also runs at normal priority, so macOS can stop the thread to run something else, which would make some blocks a lot slower.

To tell those apart, I needed every block's time instead of a summary, so I had the benchmark write each duration to a CSV in the order it ran. I also added an option to raise the thread's QoS class to `QOS_CLASS_USER_INTERACTIVE`, which tells the scheduler the thread is important. Then I ran the 4x, 32-voice configuration 48 times. The first 8 runs each waited 45 seconds before starting so the chip could cool down, and the other 40 ran back to back. Default and interactive QoS alternated the whole time, so both saw the same heat. I also logged `pmset -g thermlog` in the background.

![Block time against block index for four runs, and the mean and p99 of all 48 runs in execution order.]({{ '/assets/images/mohg/overrun-experiment.png' | relative_url }})

| Phase | QoS | Runs | Mean of run means, µs | Total overruns | Runs with no overruns |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cool | default | 4 | 696.2 | 161 | 0 |
| Cool | interactive | 4 | 679.4 | 47 | 1 |
| Hot | default | 20 | 717.1 | 255 | 6 |
| Hot | interactive | 20 | 714.7 | 234 | 8 |

The heat effect turned out to be pretty small. The median block went from about 660 µs in the cool runs to about 715 µs in the hot ones, about 8% slower, and `pmset` didn't log any thermal events. That explains the slower repeats in the sweep, but a block has to take almost twice the median to go over budget, so it doesn't explain the overruns. The very first run, after its 45-second rest, had 128 of them.

Looking at the block times, the overruns aren't isolated spikes either. They come in bursts: the renderer runs at its usual ~700 µs, then dozens of blocks in a row take 2000-3500 µs, three to five times longer, for 20-50 ms before going back to normal. In one hot run with interactive QoS, 35 of its 43 overruns came right after another overrun. There were also a couple of single blocks that took 26 ms and 35 ms. So the overrun count in a run mostly depends on how many bursts happened to land in it, which is why it jumped around in the sweep.

Raising the QoS class didn't really help. In the hot runs, interactive QoS had 234 overruns and default had 255. The cool runs look better for interactive, but there are only 4 runs on each side and one default run accounts for 128 of its 161.

I still don't know what's slowing the thread during those bursts. Running three to five times slower for a stretch could mean macOS moved it to an efficiency core, or that it was sharing its core with something else, and elapsed time alone can't separate those. The real audio thread also doesn't run at a QoS class. CoreAudio gives it a real-time scheduling policy, so the next thing I want to try is running the benchmark with that policy and seeing whether the bursts go away. After that, counting context switches per block or looking at Instruments' System Trace would show where the thread was during a burst. Until then, I'm using the mean crossing as the capacity number.

## Getting notes into the audio thread

The first thing was getting a sine wave to play using Apple's AudioToolbox. CoreAudio calls my render callback with a buffer, I fill it with the next samples, and return. If the callback runs late, you hear a glitch, so anything that can wait an unbounded amount of time (locks, allocation, blocking I/O) doesn't belong on that thread.

After that, I used CoreMIDI to connect my keyboard. `MIDIInputPortCreateWithProtocol` takes an Objective-C block as its receive callback, and Odin procedures aren't blocks, so I wrote a small C bridge. The block just captures my Odin procedure and a context pointer and calls it:

```c
return MIDIInputPortCreateWithProtocol(
    client, portName, protocol, outPort,
    ^(const MIDIEventList *eventList, void *sourceContext) {
        receiveProc(eventList, context, sourceContext);
    });
```

The receive block runs on its own CoreMIDI thread ([Apple's documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremidi/midiinputportcreatewithprotocol)), so now I had two threads touching the synth. I wanted the audio thread to own all of the live synth state. The MIDI callback decodes Note On, Note Off, and Control Change messages and copies them into a fixed-capacity single-producer/single-consumer (SPSC) queue, and the audio callback drains that queue before rendering each block.

SPSC means exactly one thread writes and one thread reads, which lets the queue be a circular array with two counters. The producer side is small enough to show in full:

```odin
tail := intrinsics.atomic_load_explicit(&q.tail, .Relaxed)
head := intrinsics.atomic_load_explicit(&q.head, .Acquire)
if tail - head == u64(N) {
    return false
}
q.buffer[tail & u64(N - 1)] = item
intrinsics.atomic_store_explicit(&q.tail, tail + 1, .Release)
return true
```

Only the producer changes `tail`, so it can read its own counter with relaxed ordering. It publishes the new `tail` with a release store, and the consumer reads it with an acquire load, which makes sure the event contents are visible before the consumer reads the slot. The consumer does the same thing with `head`, so the producer never reuses a slot that's still being read. I turn a counter into an index with `counter & (N - 1)`, which works because the capacity has to be a power of two. The MIDI queue holds 512 events.

If the queue is full, the push returns `false` instead of waiting. Right now the MIDI callback ignores that return value, so an overflow could drop a Note Off and leave a note stuck. That's something I still need a policy for.

## Notes, clicks, and envelopes

At first I could only play one note, and pressing several keys at once behaved pretty inconsistently. To see how other synths handled it, I tried a few monophonic synths in Logic Pro. They all had last-note priority: if you play a C and then a G, the G plays, and if you let go of the G, the C comes back. Letting go of the C while holding the G does nothing.

That meant I needed to track every held note and the order I pressed them in. There are only 128 possible notes, so I just used a fixed-size array and shifted the remaining notes down by hand when one was released. Removing a note this way is linear, but with an array that small, it didn't seem worth using anything more complicated.

The next problem was a clicky transient at the start of each note. The click came from the sample value jumping suddenly when the gate turned on or off, so I added short fades in and out. Then I replaced those with an ADSR envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release). The standard way to implement one seemed to be a state machine, so each voice tracks which stage it's in along with its current amplitude.

## Parameters from the knobs

I wanted to hear the sound change while turning a knob, like hearing the highs get cut as I turn down the cutoff. If the parameters change while the audio thread is reading them, that's a data race, and a mutex could block the audio thread and cause glitches. So I added a second SPSC queue for parameter events.

I didn't want to copy and paste the MIDI queue, so I made the queue generic with Odin's parametric polymorphism. Odin monomorphizes it, which basically means it generates a concrete version for each type and capacity at compile time, so there's no runtime cost. I used tagged unions for the event types:

```odin
Parameter_Event :: union {
    Ladder_Filter_Cutoff_Event,
    Ladder_Filter_Resonance_Event,
    Ladder_Filter_Drive_Event,
}
```

Later I mapped the MiniLab's knobs to cutoff, resonance, and drive. In the current MIDI path, the audio thread itself turns those Control Change messages into parameter events, so this particular use of the parameter queue doesn't actually cross threads. The changes apply at block boundaries, and I haven't added smoothing yet.

## Polyphony and unison

For polyphony, each voice computes its own sample and the synth sums them. Each voice has its own envelope, amplitude, phase, and filters. I added a `max_voices` setting, and if you play more notes than that, the synth steals the oldest voice and gives it the new note. The voices live in a fixed-size array, and the oldest voice is just `voices[0]`, so stealing was pretty straightforward. A voice stays in the pool until its release finishes, since it's still audible after the key goes up.

Unison gives each voice several oscillators that share its envelope and filters but have slightly different tuning and phase. With sine waves, the detuned oscillators beat against each other, which is when two waves with very similar frequencies drift in and out of phase. It sounded like a flanger. I spread the oscillators across the stereo field with panning: I map a position between -1 and 1 to an angle and use its cosine and sine as the left and right gains. That meant keeping separate left and right samples, and since each channel now has a different input, each voice needs separate left and right filters too.

To debug a click without printing from the audio thread, I also added a telemetry path. The audio thread pushes small records (event kind, frame, voice ID, note, amplitude) into a 256-entry SPSC queue, and a normal-priority thread prints them. If the queue fills, the audio thread drops the record and counts the loss instead of waiting.

## Aliasing

After adding unison, I noticed the high notes on some waveforms had a metallic ringing sound. That's aliasing. A saw wave has harmonics at every integer multiple of its fundamental, and at a 48 kHz sample rate anything above the 24 kHz Nyquist limit can't be represented, so it folds back into the lower range. For an A7 saw at 3520 Hz, the fourteenth harmonic is at 49,280 Hz, which folds down to 1280 Hz, below the note itself.

I routed the synth into Logic and looked at it with SPAN. With one unison oscillator on an A7 saw, you can see peaks below the fundamental:

![SPAN spectrum of an uncorrected 3520 Hz saw, with peaks below its fundamental.]({{ '/assets/images/mohg/saw-before-polyblep.png' | relative_url }})

Anti-aliasing algorithms generally smooth out the discontinuities in a waveform, and different waveforms need different corrections. Saw and square use polyBLEP, and the square needs two corrections because it has two jumps per period. After adding polyBLEP, the peaks below the fundamental got much smaller:

![SPAN spectrum of the same saw after polyBLEP.]({{ '/assets/images/mohg/saw-after-polyblep.png' | relative_url }})

The two plots use different vertical scales, so I'm only using them to show where the peaks are. The triangle uses polyBLAMP, and it still has a frequency below the fundamental starting around E7 (about 2637 Hz). At high notes, its four correction windows can overlap, which messes up the correction.

## The nonlinear filter and oversampling

The ladder filter has four stages with feedback from the last stage back to the input, and I saturate each stage with `tanh`. The feedback makes it circular: the output depends on the current input, but the input depends on feedback from the current output. I solve that with a zero-delay-feedback formulation and Newton's method. Each iteration runs a candidate input through all four stages, and it stops when the residual is below `1e-6` or after eight iterations. I start each solve from the previous sample's solution.

After adding it, I wanted to check the aliasing, and at first I couldn't see any. The issue was that I wasn't driving the filter hard enough. Most of my samples were small for volume reasons, and when the input is small, `tanh(u)` is approximately `u`, so it barely did anything nonlinear. I multiplied the samples by 30 before the filter and scaled them back down afterward, and then the aliasing showed up. This is an E7, with frequencies below the fundamental:

![Spectrum of a driven E7 through the nonlinear ladder filter, with aliased components below the fundamental.]({{ '/assets/images/mohg/nonlinear-filter-spectrum.png' | relative_url }})

That motivated oversampling. The way it works is:

1. Generate $$M$$ samples for each output sample, where $$M$$ is the oversampling factor.
2. Run the nonlinear processing at that higher rate.
3. Low-pass filter at the original Nyquist frequency.
4. Keep every $$M$$th sample.

At 4x, the oscillators and filters run at 192 kHz. The low-pass filter and downsampling step is called the decimator. Envelopes still update once per output frame. The render loop ended up looking like this:

```text
for each output frame:
    update oscillator drift
    for each oversampled step:
        for each voice:
            render and sum its unison oscillators
            normalize, saturate, and add noise
            process left and right ladder filters
            apply voice amplitude and add to the mix
        push the mixed sample into each decimator history
    calculate the retained stereo output
    update envelopes and remove finished voices
```

Since oversampling repeats everything inside the voice loop, it gets expensive fast. In my first oversampled version, 32 voices took:

| Oversampling | Mean µs/block | p99 µs/block |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1x | 306.72 | 322.00 |
| 2x | 633.10 | 726.00 |
| 4x | 1209.89 | 1245.12 |
| 8x | 2433.44 | 2550.96 |

After some testing, overruns started at about 115-116 voices at 1x, 64-65 at 2x, 34-35 at 4x, and 16-17 at 8x. So at 4x, 32 voices was right at the edge.

### How much oversampling helps

Since 4x was costing me so much, I wanted to check how much aliasing it actually removed, so I measured it at 1x, 2x, and 4x on the same commit as the final sweep below. My `alias` command renders one sustained voice through the full `synth_render` path. It tunes the tone so it lands exactly on an FFT bin and takes a 65,536-sample Hann-windowed FFT of the left channel. I added a summary to it: for harmonics 2 through 63 that are above the 24 kHz Nyquist, it works out the bin each one folds to and adds up the power at those bins, relative to the fundamental.

I only counted those bins because every voice adds a little white noise. Summing every bin that isn't a harmonic would mostly measure that noise, and the decimator filters out more of it at higher oversampling factors, which would make oversampling look better than it is. The tool also renders a reference at 8 times the output rate, and I read the same bins there as a rough floor.

I drove the filter at 4 with a 20 kHz cutoff and no resonance, which are the `alias` command's defaults. The cost column is the 32-voice, 16-unison workload from the final sweep.

| Oversampling | 5 kHz sine, dB | 3.52 kHz saw, dB | 32 voices, µs/block |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1x | -51.6 | -29.9 | 339.02 |
| 2x | -68.9 | -38.3 | 415.91 |
| 4x | -73.7 | -44.9 | 670.01 |

With the sine, the oscillator doesn't alias on its own, so everything here comes from the saturating mixer and the filter. 2x removed 17.3 dB, and 4x only removed another 4.8 dB. At 4x, the same bins in the reference read -81.9 dB, so there wasn't much left to remove. With the saw, the oscillator's polyBLEP correction leaves some aliasing of its own, and 4x kept helping: 8.4 dB from 1x to 2x, then another 6.6 dB from 2x to 4x. Going from 2x to 4x costs 1.61x the render time.

So whether 2x is enough depends on the sound. For a sine through the filter, 2x gets most of the benefit for much less time. For a bright saw, 4x still buys a few dB. I've only measured one frequency for each waveform, at a much higher drive than my benchmark's 1.0, so I want to check more notes and drive settings before choosing a default. The summary is in commit [`2c9f8d3`](https://github.com/athaapa/mohg/commit/2c9f8d3a607589df0ee4a2847fb78225afcf6ff4), which only changes the benchmark, and the [raw outputs]({{ '/assets/data/mohg/alias-2026-10-22/README.txt' | relative_url }}) are with the sweep data.

To reproduce one row from that commit:

```sh
build/bench alias --waveform saw --frequency 3520 --oversampling 2 --samples 65536
```

## Measuring the renderer

I made an offline benchmark that calls the same `synth_render` procedure as the audio callback. It sends Note On events during setup, uses a fixed random seed for the oscillator phases, runs 100 warmup blocks, and then times each measured block with `mach_absolute_time`. It reports the mean, p50, p99, and the number of blocks over 1333.33 µs. The filter is set to 15 kHz cutoff, 0.9 resonance, and 1.0 drive, and I build with `-o:speed` (plus `-debug` for profiling).

I count an offline overrun whenever a measured block takes more than 1333.33 µs. These elapsed times include time the OS spends running other work during the call. The benchmark runs as a normal process and leaves out the live callback's MIDI handling, so I use it to compare render workloads. A live callback measurement would need its own capture.

For the final sweep, I built one executable from an archive of commit `ed7293a84d9044f143beaea6afada5b67abb3816`, using `odin version dev-2026-09:a2fb372b7` and `-o:speed -debug`. I ran 5000 measured blocks after 100 warmup blocks for each configuration, with 16 unison oscillators, a saw waveform, and the filter settings above. The seed is 1, detune is 15 cents, and chorus is off. I kept the executable unchanged throughout the sweep and recorded its SHA-256 in the manifest.

I started with 8, 16, 32, 48, 64, 80, 96, 112, and 128 voices, running 1x, 2x, and 4x at each count. For the capacity table, I interpolated between those first-pass points. For the zero-overrun search, I then tested individual counts down from just below the next coarse count above its highest zero-overrun result. I rejected a count after any observed overrun, and stopped when a count passed three runs. I didn't test every count above the one that passed, so it's the result of this search, and repeating it can give a different count. I kept the failed runs in the data too.

To reproduce one configuration after building the archived commit:

```sh
build/bench render --voices 32 --unison 16 --oversampling 4 \
  --frames 64 --blocks 5000 --warmup 100 \
  --cutoff 15000 --resonance 0.9 --drive 1
```

The [CSV]({{ '/assets/data/mohg/final-sweep-2026-10-22/runs.csv' | relative_url }}), [manifest]({{ '/assets/data/mohg/final-sweep-2026-10-22/manifest.json' | relative_url }}), and [full measurement bundle]({{ '/assets/data/mohg/final-sweep-2026-10-22/evidence.zip' | relative_url }}) include the refinement runs, raw reports, and scripts.

For more detail, I used Instruments' CPU Counters. For the full 32-voice, 4x workload over 5000 blocks, I got about 124 billion instructions and 20.8 billion cycles, which gives an IPC of about 5.94. A scaling benchmark varies one setting at a time and wraps each measured loop in a signpost interval, and a script uses `xctrace` exports to match the counter samples to each configuration.

### Splitting up the loop costs

There are a few nested loops in `synth_render`, and I wanted to know how much each one contributed. I modeled the total cycles as

$$
C = FLB\,[n(ka+b)+c]+FBd
$$

where $$F$$ is frames per block, $$L$$ is the oversampling factor, $$B$$ is measured blocks, $$n$$ is voices, and $$k$$ is unison oscillators. $$a$$ is the cost per oscillator iteration, $$b$$ per voice per oversampled step (mostly filtering), $$c$$ per oversampled step (mostly the decimators), and $$d$$ per output frame.

Holding everything else fixed, I swept $$k = 2, 4, 8, 16$$ to get $$a$$ from the slope, swept $$n = 8, 16, 32$$ to get $$ka + b$$, and ran zero voices at 1x and 4x to separate $$c$$ and $$d$$. My first rough estimate from the slopes, at 32 voices and 16 unison, was:

| Work | Cost per item | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Oscillator iteration | 22 cycles | 70% |
| Voice level | 110 cycles | 22% |
| Subframe level | 1150 cycles | 8% |

Later I had a script fit all the runs at once, which put the oscillator cost at 29.3 cycles.

The model assumes these costs just add up, which isn't exactly how the core works, and some points were a few percent off the fit. $$d$$ also came out slightly negative, which can't be a real cost and is worth looking into. So I used the numbers to decide where to look rather than as exact costs.

## Caching oscillator calculations

The oscillators were doing a lot of work, about 125 instructions per iteration. In theory each oscillator is independent, so I wanted to check the assembly to see whether that independence was being used, and whether anything in the loop could be hoisted out.

Looking at the assembly, the waveform generators and their anti-aliasing code were inlined, and SIMD wasn't being used. The most expensive line in the oscillator loop was the panning: `left_gain := math.cos(pan_angle)` and `right_gain := math.sin(pan_angle)`. It accounted for about 8 billion cycles, more than double the next line, which works out to 12.24 cycles per oscillator iteration. To see how much it mattered, I temporarily replaced both with `pan_angle`, and the mean time per block went from 1230 µs to 830 µs. `pan_angle` doesn't change while a note plays, so the loop was computing the same sine and cosine over and over.

The next most expensive line was the frequency calculation, `voice.frequency * cents_to_freq_multiplier(detune + drift)`, at about 3 billion cycles, or 4.62 cycles per iteration. The frequency doesn't change during a note either. A few other things showed up too: the waveform switch took about 1.5 billion cycles, the saw generation about 1 billion (mostly polyBLEP), and the detune calculation about 900 million, mostly on a `ucvtf` instruction (42%) and an `ldr` (56%).

For scale, at 32 voices, 16 unison, 4x oversampling, 64 frames, and 5000 blocks, the oscillator loop runs

$$
32\cdot16\cdot4\cdot64\cdot5000=655{,}360{,}000
$$

times, so anything small in that loop adds up quickly. I cached each oscillator's frequency, pan angle, left and right gains, and phase step on `note_on`, so panning became just two multiplies. One thing to keep in mind with caching is that anything that changes those values has to refresh the cache. My alias tool set `voice.frequency` after note setup, and the oscillators kept the old frequency until I fixed that.

| Oversampling | Mean before, µs/block | Mean after, µs/block | p99 after, µs/block |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1x | 306.72 | 121.64 | 133.71 |
| 2x | 633.10 | 239.32 | 267.12 |
| 4x | 1209.89 | 519.33 | 1334.46 |
| 8x | 2433.44 | 976.53 | 1005.50 |

The fitted oscillator cost went from 29.3 to 7.7 cycles. The 4x run still had 11 overruns in 1000 blocks, though, so its p99 was over budget even with a mean under half of it. I don't have enough information from that run to explain those long blocks.
## Replacing `tanh`

After caching, I added saturation in every ladder stage, oscillator drift, a saturating mixer before the filter, and a noise source. That changed where the time went:

| Work | Cost per item | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Oscillator iteration | ~10 cycles | 27% |
| Voice level | ~400 cycles | 63% |
| Subframe level | ~1000 cycles | 5% |

`ladder_filter_process` was now 53.3% of the total cycles. Inside `ladder_forward`, `tanh` calls accounted for about 5 of its 12 billion cycles, so the first thing I tried was a fast approximation:

```odin
fast_tanh :: proc(x: f64) -> f64 {
    x2 := x * x
    return x * (27 + x2) / (27 + 9 * x2)
}
```

### Checking the microbenchmark

Before switching, I wanted to know how many cycles each version took. My first results were pretty suspicious: `fast_tanh` took exactly as many "cycles" as reading the cycle counter. It turned out I was running without optimizations, and my volatile loads and stores had some overhead. After fixing that, `fast_tanh` came out at 0.68 cycles per call, which was honestly even more suspicious.

I looked at the assembly in `lldb`, and the counter read was:

```asm
mrs x20, CNTVCT_EL0
```

`CNTVCT_EL0` is ARM's virtual timer count, which isn't the same as CPU cycles. Using `intrinsics.read_cycle_counter_frequency()`, I found the counter runs at 1 GHz, so I switched everything to nanoseconds. Sweeping the input over 50,000 iterations gave:

| Input | `math.tanh`, ns/iteration | `fast_tanh`, ns/iteration |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0.1 | 2.980 | 0.673 |
| 0.6 | 2.954 | 0.683 |
| 0.7 | 7.456 | 0.636 |
| 1.0 | 8.984 | 0.678 |

`tanh` jumps between 0.6 and 0.7, and in the assembly I saw that Odin switches to a different calculation for values above 0.625.

### Predicting the speedup

`fast_tanh` was at least 4x faster in the microbenchmark, but I didn't expect quite that much in the filter. The microbenchmark lets independent calls overlap, while in the ladder each stage depends on the last. `fast_tanh` still has an `fdiv`, which I read has a latency of about 14 cycles. `fast_tanh` came out to around 3-5 cycles per call (assuming 4.4 GHz) and `tanh` about 40, so I thought the filter would mostly be latency bound after switching. The slow throughput of `tanh` had probably been hiding most of that latency. Going from about 40 cycles to 14, I predicted about 60% fewer cycles for the `tanh` work.
My prediction was slightly off. It gave about 53% fewer cycles for the `tanh` operations. Total cycles dropped to about 15 billion with about 60 billion instructions, an IPC around 4. On the full renderer:

| Nonlinearity | Mean µs/block | p50 µs/block | p99 µs/block | Overruns |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `math.tanh` | 1127.36 | 1128.75 | 1173.17 | 0/5000 |
| `fast_tanh` | 639.95 | 637.17 | 683.58 | 0/5000 |

That's about a 1.76x speedup. It does change the filter's sound, though, and the Newton step still uses `1 - t*t` as the derivative, which is exact for `tanh` but not for `fast_tanh`. I still need to check residuals and convergence before treating it as a drop-in replacement. A wrong derivative can also make Newton's method take more iterations, so I want to log iteration counts before and after fixing it.

After that, `ladder_forward` was still slow. Each iteration depends on the last, and I don't think I can make it much faster without changing the algorithm. That needs more DSP knowledge than I have right now, so I left it alone.

### A change I couldn't explain

I ran the scaling benchmark again, and both the voice and oscillator costs dropped. The oscillator cost dropped by half, from about 10.7 to 5.4 cycles, which was interesting because I didn't think this change should've affected it that much.

In Instruments, something looked very wrong. With `fast_tanh`, `synth_render`'s call to `oscillator_advance_phase` went from 2.5 billion cycles (10.4% of `synth_render`) to less than 10 million (0.1%). That call only runs if `audible` is `true`, and `audible` comes from the voice's amplitude, which only the envelope controls. So I strongly suspected something was messing with `audible`, even though `fast_tanh` is only used inside the filter. I tested it and it seemed fine. It's still a mystery, but I didn't want to spend too much time on it since it wouldn't change what I'd do next.

Later, I checked the disassembly of the current build, which uses `fast_tanh`. There's no separate `oscillator_advance_phase` function in it at all, and `synth_render` never calls one, so the compiler inlined it. Instruments would then count those cycles under `synth_render` instead of `oscillator_advance_phase`, which would explain the drop. My guess is that the `math.tanh` build still called it as a separate function, but I haven't rebuilt that version to check.

## Trying more accumulators

The next thing I looked at was the oscillator loop:

```odin
for osc_idx in 0 ..< synth.unison_count {
    osc := &voice.oscillators[osc_idx]
    voice_sample += oscillator_render(osc, synth.waveform)
    if audible {
        oscillator_advance_phase(osc)
    }
}
```

Technically each oscillator can be computed independently, and they only need to be summed, so I thought I could vectorize it. First, though, I wanted to make sure the dependency on `voice_sample` was actually worth optimizing, so I tried four accumulators.

| Variant | Mean µs/block |
| --- | ---: |
| One accumulator | 643 |
| Four accumulators | 669 |
| Four accumulators, forced inline | 646 |

With four accumulators, it got slower. Looking at the assembly, the compiler had stopped inlining `oscillator_render` and was saving values to the stack between calls:

```asm
bl   "_mohg::oscillator_render"
...
str  q0, [sp, #192]
str  q1, [sp, #144]
bl   "_mohg::oscillator_render"
```

After forcing it to inline, the four-accumulator version was about the same as one accumulator. That gives me little evidence that the accumulation dependency is limiting performance in this configuration. I also found some packed SIMD instructions, like `fmul.2d v0, v1, v0[0]`, but that one just multiplies one oscillator's sample by its left and right gains. The waveform math still runs one oscillator at a time, so vectorizing across oscillators is still open.

## Calculating outputs I threw away

The last thing I looked into was the decimator. It's probably the component I understand the least in my system, and it was taking a few billion cycles, so I thought it was worth investigating.

My decimator is a 257-tap FIR low-pass filter. FIR means each output is a weighted sum of the most recent inputs, and a tap is one weight times one input. I use a Hamming-windowed sinc with a 21 kHz cutoff for the weights, and each channel keeps its last 257 inputs in a circular buffer:

```odin
for k in 0 ..< FIR_TAPS {
    idx := d.write - 1 - k
    if idx < 0 {
        idx += FIR_TAPS
    }
    result += d.coeffs[k] * d.history[idx]
}
```

The first thing I noticed was that I called `decimator_output` on every oversampled step but only kept the result on the last one. At 4x, I was calculating four outputs per channel and throwing three away. `decimator_output` doesn't change any state, so I could push every sample but only calculate the output on the last step:

```odin
push_decimator(oversample, &synth.left_decimator, &synth.right_decimator)

if i == synth.oversampling_factor - 1 {
    oversample = apply_decimator(
        oversample,
        &synth.left_decimator,
        &synth.right_decimator,
    )
}
```

For a 64-frame stereo block at 4x, that goes from 512 FIR outputs to 128. Arm's [CMSIS-DSP FIR decimator](https://arm-software.github.io/CMSIS-DSP/main/group__FIR__decimate.html) does the same thing. `decimator_output` had been 10.9% of the cycles, so removing 75% of it works out to about 8.2% if nothing else changed.

| Decimator | Mean µs/block | p50 µs/block | p99 µs/block | Overruns |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Output every oversampled step | 723.34 | 716.17 | 759.67 | 0/5000 |
| Output only on the last step | 668.48 | 661.17 | 705.96 | 0/5000 |

The mean fell by 54.86 µs, about 7.6%. In Instruments, `decimator_output` went from about 1.43 billion cycles to 0.35 billion. The total cycles in that capture were higher, though, and I haven't matched the capture conditions, so I'm using the elapsed time for the comparison. Since calculating an output doesn't change any state, the outputs I keep should be identical to before, but I haven't compared them sample by sample yet.

## Where I am now

In the final sweep, the first 32-voice, 16-unison, 4x run averaged 670.01 µs per block, about half the 1333.33 µs budget. By where the mean crosses the budget, 4x capacity went from about 35 voices to about 59, and 2x from about 67 to about 78. Oversampling at 2x removed most of the aliasing from a driven sine, while a bright saw still gained a few dB from 4x.

The biggest open question is still the overruns. They come in bursts where the thread runs three to five times slower for 20-50 ms, heat only accounts for about an 8% slowdown, and raising the QoS class didn't fix them. Next, I want to run the benchmark with the real-time scheduling policy CoreAudio uses for the audio thread, and measure the live callback itself, since that's what actually decides whether I hear a glitch.

The decimator is the other thing I want to come back to. A 257-tap linear-phase FIR delays the signal by 128 input samples, which is about 0.67 ms at 192 kHz, or half a buffer of extra latency. A cascade of halfband filters could cut both that delay and the work. I'd also like to look at the hot `subs` and `ldr` again, since the wraparound check in the history loop is a likely suspect, and a history buffer that stores each sample twice would remove it.

Smaller checks: comparing the decimator outputs before and after, checking the filter approximation's convergence, measuring aliasing at more notes and drive settings, and trying to vectorize the oscillators now that I know the accumulator isn't the problem.
